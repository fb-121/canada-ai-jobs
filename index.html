Data sources

Occupations follow the National Occupational Classification (NOC) 2021. The 113 occupations shown are NOC unit groups chosen to represent every major sector of the economy. They are not all 516 unit groups.

Workers are counts from Statistics Canada's 2021 Census of people who worked full year, full time and reported employment income in 2020. Part-time, part-year and seasonal workers are not counted, so figures are lower than total headcount.

Median pay comes from the ESDC Labour Market Information wage dataset (national figures, released November 2025). Hourly wages are converted to annual pay at 2,080 hours a year (40 hours a week for 52 weeks). One exception: the Census groups senior managers (NOC 00011 to 00015) into a single category that has no ESDC wage, so its figure is the Census median employment income for 2020.

Education is the TEER category built into every NOC 2021 code (its second digit). It describes the training, education, experience and responsibility usually needed to enter the occupation.

Limitations

  • All figures are national. Alberta's resource economy and Ontario's finance and tech sectors have different profiles that a national view flattens.
  • Employment reflects 2020, a year affected by COVID-19, so counts in healthcare, hospitality and food service may be atypical. It is still the most recent detailed occupational count in Canada.
  • Pay reflects 2025 while employment reflects 2020. Each measure uses the most current reliable source available.
  • This is an independent analysis. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by Statistics Canada or ESDC.

How AI exposure is scored

Each occupation gets one score from 0 to 10: how much AI is likely to reshape the work over the next 5 to 10 years. The score counts both direct effects (AI doing tasks people do today) and indirect ones (AI making each worker productive enough that fewer are needed). The main signal is whether the work product is digital. Physical, hands-on and face-to-face work has a natural barrier.

The score measures digital AI only. Robots, self-checkouts and self-driving vehicles are a separate, slower kind of automation, so they do not raise a score. That is why drivers and warehouse workers score low.

0 to 1Minimal: physical work in unpredictable environments
2 to 3Low: AI touches only peripheral tasks
4 to 5Moderate: AI assists, but human presence stays central
6 to 7High: mostly knowledge work, strongly AI-assisted
8 to 9Very high: almost entirely digital work, major restructuring
10Maximum: routine digital processing AI can already do

Scores were produced in September 2026 by Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic), which applied the prompt below, unchanged, to each occupation's official NOC 2021 title and definition. Every score and its rationale is published in the GitHub repository. The March 2026 version of this site used scores assigned without a fixed prompt; those have been fully replaced. These are informed estimates, not predictions, and reasonable analysts could score some occupations differently.

View the scoring prompt
You are an expert analyst evaluating how exposed Canadian occupations are to AI. You will be given an occupation's official NOC 2021 title and definition.

Rate the occupation's overall AI Exposure on a scale from 0 to 10.

AI Exposure measures how much AI will reshape this occupation over the next 5 to 10 years. Consider both direct effects (AI performing tasks humans do today) and indirect effects (AI making each worker so productive that fewer are needed).

The key signal is whether the job's core work product is fundamentally digital. If the job can be done entirely from a computer (writing, coding, analyzing, communicating, processing records), exposure is inherently high, because AI capability in digital work is advancing quickly. Jobs that require physical presence, manual skill, or real-time human interaction in the physical world have a natural barrier. Score digital AI only: do not raise a score for robotics or autonomous vehicles, which are separate and slower forms of automation.

Calibration anchors:
0-1: Minimal. Almost entirely physical or hands-on work in unpredictable environments. Examples: roofer, landscaping labourer, commercial fisher.
2-3: Low. Mostly physical or interpersonal work; AI touches only peripheral tasks such as scheduling or paperwork. Examples: electrician, plumber, paramedic, dental hygienist.
4-5: Moderate. A mix of physical or interpersonal work and knowledge work; AI assists the information parts but human presence remains central. Examples: registered nurse, general practitioner, police officer.
6-7: High. Predominantly knowledge work with real need for judgment, relationships or presence; workers using AI become substantially more productive. Examples: teacher, manager, accountant, lawyer.
8-9: Very high. Almost all core tasks are done on a computer in domains where AI is improving rapidly; the occupation faces major restructuring. Examples: software developer, graphic designer, translator, paralegal.
10: Maximum. Routine, fully digital information processing with no physical component; AI can already do most of it today. Examples: data entry clerk, medical transcriptionist.

A high score does not predict that the job will disappear. It measures how much the work will change.

Respond with ONLY a JSON object: {"exposure": <0-10>, "rationale": "<2 sentences on the key factors>"}
